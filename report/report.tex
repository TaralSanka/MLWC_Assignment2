\section{SVM for LOS/NLOS Identification}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

Each realisation has six complex taps with delays. The tap amplitudes and powers are

\begin{equation}
|h_l| = \sqrt{h_{\mathrm{real},l}^2 + h_{\mathrm{imag},l}^2},
\qquad P_l = |h_l|^2, \qquad l = 0,\dots,L-1,\quad L = 6 .
\end{equation}

Five features are extracted per realisation. Each is a statistic over the six taps of one
snapshot, so all moments are population statistics ($\texttt{ddof}=0$).

\subsubsection{Kurtosis}
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{K} = \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(|h| - \mu_{|h|}\right)^4\right]}{\sigma_{|h|}^4}
\end{equation}
Peakedness of the amplitude distribution. LOS has one dominant tap, so it is more peaked.

\subsubsection{Skewness}
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S} = \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(|h| - \mu_{|h|}\right)^3\right]}{\sigma_{|h|}^3}
\end{equation}
Asymmetry of the same distribution. Rayleigh (NLOS) and Rician (LOS) differ in asymmetry.

\subsubsection{Rising Time}
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begin{equation}
\Delta\tau = \tau\!\left(\arg\max_l |h_l|\right) - \min_l \tau_l
\end{equation}
Delay gap between the first path and the strongest path. Zero in LOS, where the first path is
the strongest.

\subsubsection{RMS Delay Spread}
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begin{equation}
\tau_m = \frac{\sum_l \tau_l P_l}{\sum_l P_l},
\qquad
\tau_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_l (\tau_l - \tau_m)^2 P_l}{\sum_l P_l}}
\end{equation}
Spread of power over delay, in ns. Small in LOS (power concentrated on tap~0), large in NLOS.

\subsubsection{Rician $K$-Factor}
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begin{equation}
K_r = \frac{|h|^2_{\max}}{2\,\sigma^2_{|h|}}
\label{eq:kfactor}
\end{equation}
Dominant tap power normalised by the variance of the tap amplitudes, exactly as defined in the
reference paper. This choice has a consequence, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:kdiscussion}.

\subsubsection{Discussion}

\paragraph{Which single feature performs best, and why?}
RMS delay spread, at every SNR (88.50\% at 0~dB to 98.75\% at 30~dB). The generator draws LOS
delays from an exponential of mean 30~ns and NLOS from one of mean 100~ns, so the classes
differ both in how power is spread over the taps and in the span of delays those taps occupy.
$\tau_{\mathrm{rms}}$ captures both at once, giving class means of 45.19~ns against 6.98~ns.
The other four features use only the amplitude distribution and discard the delay axis.

\paragraph{Does combining all five features help, and where most?}
Yes, at every SNR. The gain of SVM-6 over the best single feature is $+0.75$, $+8.25$,
$+6.50$, $+2.50$, $+1.75$, $+1.75$ and $+1.25$ percentage points at 0--30~dB.

It is largest at 5 and 10~dB, where the delay-domain feature is already useful and the
amplitude-domain features have just become usable; the two families make different mistakes,
so their errors are weakly correlated. At 0~dB the other features are too noisy to contribute,
and above 20~dB $\tau_{\mathrm{rms}}$ alone is already at 98\%, leaving no headroom.

\paragraph{Which feature fails at low SNR despite being physically meaningful?}
\label{sec:kdiscussion}
The Rician $K$-factor: 60.00\% at 0~dB, the weakest of the five. Two mechanisms combine.

\emph{Low-SNR failure.} The noise variance is $1/(L\cdot\mathrm{SNR}) = 0.167$ per tap at
0~dB, while the five scattered taps of a LOS realisation carry only about $0.02$ each. The
noise is roughly eight times stronger than the taps it sits on, so those taps report the noise
floor instead of the channel and the LOS statistics migrate onto the NLOS ones
(Table~\ref{tab:drift}). This hits kurtosis, skewness and the $K$-factor alike, which is why
all three sit at 58--60\% at 0~dB. Only $\tau_{\mathrm{rms}}$ survives, since its separation
rests on the noiseless $\tau$ columns.

\centering
\caption{Feature drift with SNR, as LOS mean $\rightarrow$ NLOS mean.}
\label{tab:drift}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\emph{A second, SNR-independent problem.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:kfactor} divides by the variance of the
tap amplitudes. With $L=6$ and one dominant tap of amplitude $a$,
$\sigma^2_{|h|} \approx a^2(L-1)/L^2$, so $K_r \approx L^2/[2(L-1)] = 3.6$ however strong the
LOS component is. The estimator saturates, which is why the LOS mean falls below the NLOS mean
and the feature plateaus near 82\% even at 30~dB. Repeating the experiment with the textbook
form $K = P_{\mathrm{peak}}/(P_{\mathrm{total}} - P_{\mathrm{peak}})$ confirms this is the
definition and not the physics:

\subsubsection{Discussion}

\paragraph{At high SNR, do the two curves agree?}
Yes --- identical at 25 and 30~dB and within 0.25~pp at 15 and 20~dB. Above roughly 12~dB the
features are effectively SNR-invariant: from Table~\ref{tab:drift}, the LOS kurtosis moves only
3.83 to 3.87 between 15 and 30~dB and the rising time stays at zero. The same region of feature
space is occupied at both SNRs, so a boundary fitted at 25~dB is already correct and retraining
has nothing to adapt to.

\paragraph{At low SNR, which strategy is better, and by how much?}
Matched training, by $+31.25$~pp at 0~dB and $+20.00$~pp at 5~dB. At 0~dB the fixed model is
only 8~pp above a coin flip.

The cause is covariate shift. The 25~dB model learned a boundary for clusters at
(kurtosis 3.87, $\tau_{\mathrm{rms}}$ 7.2~ns) and (2.16, 45.5~ns); at 0~dB those clusters have
moved to (2.55, 20.5~ns) and (2.17, 65.4~ns), so LOS test points land where the model expects
NLOS. The frozen scaler compounds this, normalising 0~dB features by 25~dB statistics so they
arrive outside the region where the local RBF kernel has support.

\paragraph{Would training at 0~dB and testing across all SNR differ?}
Yes, and not symmetrically. As SNR rises, feature values move monotonically \emph{inward}: the
broad, overlapping 0~dB clusters contract, and the tight high-SNR clusters sit nested inside
that overlap at its two extremes. A boundary drawn through the middle of the smeared 0~dB
distributions therefore still falls between the high-SNR clusters --- roughly right, but hedged
against noise that is no longer there. In the other direction there is no such protection,
since the 25~dB boundary sits at one extreme and the test points migrate past it.

A 0~dB model should therefore degrade gracefully rather than collapse, while never reaching the
100\% that matched training attains. Running it confirms this:

\section{Constellation Demodulation and Clustering using K-Means}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Dataset Generation}

The square 16-QAM grid uses coordinates from $\{-3,-1,1,3\}$. Its average symbol energy is
$\mathbb{E}[I^2] + \mathbb{E}[Q^2] = 10$, so every point is divided by $\sqrt{10}$ to give
$E_s = 1$:

\begin{equation}
x_I,\, x_Q \in \left\{ \tfrac{-3}{\sqrt{10}},\, \tfrac{-1}{\sqrt{10}},\,
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}},\, \tfrac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \right\}.
\end{equation}

With $E_s=1$ the noise power is $N_0 = 1/\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{lin}}$, split equally between
$I$ and $Q$. For each SNR in $\{0,5,\dots,30\}$~dB, 200 samples per constellation point are
generated, giving 3200 rows per SNR and 22{,}400 in total. Seed 67.

One number is used repeatedly below: after scaling, the nearest-neighbour symbol spacing is
$2/\sqrt{10} = 0.6325$.

At 0~dB the noise standard deviation exceeds the distance between adjacent symbols, which
explains most of what follows.

\subsection{Amplitude and Phase Features}

For each received sample $z = x_I + jx_Q$:

\begin{equation}
r = \sqrt{x_I^2 + x_Q^2},
\qquad
\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(x_Q, x_I) \ \ \text{[rad]}.
\end{equation}

The two-argument form is used because plain $\arctan(x_Q/x_I)$ returns values in
$(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ and so maps a point and its negation to the same phase, aliasing 8 of the 16
symbols. The cost is measurable: polar-feature purity at 25~dB is 1.0000 with
$\operatorname{atan2}$ against 0.5188 with plain $\arctan$.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{K-Means Clustering at SNR $=25$ dB}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

K-means is fitted on the Cartesian features for $K = 2,\dots,20$ with k-means++ initialisation,
$n_{\mathrm{init}} = 10$ and \texttt{random\_state}~$=42$. Inertia is
$J = \sum_k \sum_{x_i \in C_k} \lVert x_i - \mu_k \rVert^2$, which falls monotonically with $K$
and is read for its elbow. The silhouette coefficient is
$s(i) = [b(i) - a(i)] / \max(a(i), b(i))$, which has a genuine maximum.

\subsubsection{Comparison of Feature Sets}
\label{sec:featuresets}

\paragraph{Which feature set gives the highest purity?}
At the required 25~dB all three reach 1.0000, so the comparison is saturated: the clusters are
about sixteen noise standard deviations apart and even a distorted metric separates them.
Across the rest of the range, Set~1 (Cartesian) is highest everywhere, by up to 10~pp at 10~dB.
Set~3 finishes last because $r$ and $\theta$ are a deterministic function of $(x_I,x_Q)$ and so
add no information, only weight: after standardisation the two distorted polar axes influence
the distance metric as much as the two clean Cartesian ones.

\paragraph{Why does Euclidean distance suit Cartesian but distort unweighted polar
coordinates?}
The channel adds independent Gaussian noise of equal variance to $x_I$ and $x_Q$, so its
density contours are circles in the I/Q plane and the ML detector is the
minimum-Euclidean-distance detector. K-means assumes exactly that --- isotropic clusters
partitioned by Euclidean Voronoi boundaries --- so its bias matches the channel, and since the
grid is uniformly spaced, equal distance means equal error probability. Polar coordinates break
this in three ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Incompatible units.} $r$ spans $[0,1.34]$ and $\theta$ spans $[-\pi,\pi]$, so in
$(\Delta r)^2 + (\Delta\theta)^2$ the phase axis dominates and amplitude is under-used.
\item \textbf{$\Delta\theta$ is not a distance.} Two points at radius $r$ separated by
$\Delta\theta$ are physically $2r\sin(\Delta\theta/2)$ apart, but the metric charges
$\Delta\theta$ regardless of $r$. The inner symbols sit at $r=0.447$ and the corners at
$r=1.342$, so angular separation is exaggerated threefold near the origin.
\item \textbf{$\theta$ wraps.} $+\pi$ and $-\pi$ are the same direction but maximally distant in
Euclidean terms, so a cluster straddling the negative real axis is split.
\end{enumerate}

The result is wedge- and annulus-shaped decision regions instead of the square Voronoi cells the
channel calls for.

\paragraph{Why does fitting on low-SNR samples cause centroid merging?}
At 0~dB the per-axis $\sigma = 0.707$ exceeds the symbol spacing of 0.6325, so each Gaussian
blob is wider than the gap to its neighbour and all sixteen superpose into one broad cloud. The
density minima between symbols are gone, leaving nothing for the algorithm to lock onto.
K-means minimises within-cluster sum of squares, so it does the only thing its objective
rewards: it tiles that cloud into sixteen roughly equal-mass Voronoi cells, placed to balance
point mass rather than to sit on the true symbols. Table~\ref{tab:geometry} shows the result ---
the centroids drift an RMS 0.5744 off the grid, 91\% of a full symbol spacing, and spread
outward until their minimum gap (0.8561) exceeds the true grid's. Covering the wide noise tails
leaves the crowded interior under-served, so several true symbols share one cluster and the
majority vote inside purity counts the rest as errors.

The fixed template is immune because it is never fitted to the noisy data. Its centroids stay on
the true grid, so prediction reduces to a minimum-distance detector --- the ML detector for
AWGN. It cannot recover what the noise destroyed, but it is optimal given the noise, and so acts
as a floor the adaptive fit can match but not beat.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================

Both questions turn on how much structure a learning algorithm should estimate from noisy data
rather than be given in advance.

In Question~1 the features split into two families. RMS delay spread reads the noiseless delay
axis and is the strongest single feature at every SNR; kurtosis, skewness and the $K$-factor
read only amplitudes across six taps and collapse to 58--60\% at 0~dB, where the added noise is
about eight times stronger than the scattered taps. Combining all five reaches 100\% above
20~dB and gains most at 5--10~dB. Training at a single SNR is safe above roughly 15~dB and costs
31.25~pp at 0~dB, and the failure is one-sided: a 0~dB-trained model instead holds about 90\%
everywhere without ever reaching 100\%.

In Question~2 both the elbow and the silhouette identify $K=16$, and at 25~dB K-means recovers
the constellation exactly. Cartesian coordinates are the right representation because Euclidean
distance there is the geometry the AWGN channel imposes. Below 15~dB the fixed template beats
adaptive refitting, because once the noise exceeds the symbol spacing K-means tiles the merged
cloud by point mass and drifts its centroids almost a full symbol off the grid, while the
template stays pinned to the constellation.

In both cases, when the data are clean, estimating and imposing agree. When the data are noisy,
estimating a structure that is already known injects error rather than saving effort.
